India is one of the few countries in the world where Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) is required by law. Since 1 April 2014, larger companies have had to spend part of their profits on social development. A decade later, mandated CSR has grown from about ₹10,066 crore in FY 2014-15 to about ₹40,794 crore in FY 2024-25, according to the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA).

This guide explains how the law works, in plain English.

Where CSR law comes from

CSR in India is governed by Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013, the Companies (CSR Policy) Rules, 2014 (substantially amended in January 2021), and Schedule VII of the Act, which lists the activities that count as CSR.

Which companies must spend on CSR?

A company must comply with Section 135 if, in the immediately preceding financial year, it had any one of the following:

  • a net worth of ₹500 crore or more;
  • a turnover of ₹1,000 crore or more; or
  • a net profit of ₹5 crore or more.

We cover the thresholds in detail in our guide to CSR applicability.

How much must be spent?

Eligible companies must spend at least 2% of their average net profit from the three immediately preceding financial years. Net profit here is calculated under Section 198 of the Act. See how to calculate the 2% CSR obligation for a worked example.

What counts as CSR?

Only activities that fall within Schedule VII qualify. These include eradicating hunger and poverty, promoting health care and sanitation, education, gender equality, environmental sustainability, rural development, disaster management and more. Activities in the normal course of business, or those that benefit only employees, do not count. Our Schedule VII guide covers each category.

The CSR committee and policy

Companies covered by Section 135 must form a CSR Committee of the board. The exception is a company whose CSR obligation is ₹50 lakh or less, whose board can perform the committee's functions itself. The committee formulates a CSR policy, recommends an annual action plan and monitors implementation.

What happens to money that isn't spent?

Since 2021, unspent CSR is no longer a matter of "comply or explain".

  • Money allocated to an ongoing project must be moved to an Unspent CSR Account within 30 days of the end of the financial year and spent within three years.
  • Money that is not tied to an ongoing project must be transferred to a fund listed in Schedule VII, such as the PM CARES Fund, within six months.

Read more in our guide to unspent CSR funds.

Who can implement CSR?

Companies can implement CSR directly or through implementing agencies. External agencies must register with the MCA using Form CSR-1. Registered trusts, societies, Section 8 companies, and entities set up by the government all qualify.

Reporting and disclosure

CSR spending is disclosed in the board's report and filed with the MCA through Form CSR-2. Companies with large CSR obligations must also commission independent impact assessments of bigger projects.

The bigger picture

CSR has made India's companies one of the country's largest sources of development funding. But the money is unevenly spread. In FY 2023-24, Bihar, home to about one in eleven Indians, received only about 1% of the CSR that can be traced to a state. Understanding the law is the first step; deciding where the money goes is where the real impact lies.

Not every company in India has to spend on CSR. Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013 applies only to companies that cross specific financial thresholds. Here is how to tell whether yours does.

The three thresholds

A company is covered if, in the immediately preceding financial year, it met any one of these:

CriterionThreshold
Net worth₹500 crore or more
Turnover₹1,000 crore or more
Net profit₹5 crore or more

Crossing even one threshold brings the company under Section 135 for the current year.

Does it apply to private and foreign companies?

Yes. The law applies to every company registered in India, whether public or private, listed or unlisted. It also applies to foreign companies with a branch or project office in India, using the figures of their Indian operations.

What if a company stops meeting the criteria?

Under the amended rules, a company that ceases to meet all three thresholds for three consecutive financial years is no longer required to comply with Section 135. Until then, the obligation continues.

What it means in practice

Once covered, a company must:

  1. calculate its CSR obligation (2% of average net profit; see our calculation guide);
  2. form a CSR Committee, unless the obligation is ₹50 lakh or less;
  3. adopt a CSR policy and an annual action plan;
  4. spend through eligible activities and implementing agencies; and
  5. disclose its spending in the board's report and in Form CSR-2.

A note on newly covered companies

Companies crossing the threshold for the first time often have small obligations and little in-house CSR capacity. Many choose to fund a single, well-designed programme with an experienced partner rather than spreading small amounts across many causes. This tends to produce clearer impact and simpler reporting.

Section 135 requires eligible companies to spend at least 2% of their average net profit from the three immediately preceding financial years. The rule is simple to state but easy to get wrong. Here is how to do it.

Step 1: Find net profit for each of the last three years

"Net profit" for CSR purposes is calculated under Section 198 of the Companies Act. That is not always the same as profit before tax in the financial statements. For CSR purposes:

  • profits from overseas branches are excluded;
  • dividends received from other Indian companies that are themselves covered by Section 135 are excluded.

Step 2: Take the average

Add the three years' net profits and divide by three. Loss years count as negative numbers in the average.

Step 3: Take 2%

The CSR obligation for the current year is 2% of that average.

A worked example

Financial yearNet profit (Section 198)
FY 2022-23₹180 crore
FY 2023-24₹210 crore
FY 2024-25₹240 crore
Average₹210 crore
CSR obligation for FY 2025-26 (2%)₹4.2 crore

Adjustments to keep in mind

  • Excess spending in earlier years can be set off against the obligation for up to three following years, if the board approves it.
  • Administrative overheads may not exceed 5% of total CSR spending for the year.
  • Surplus from CSR activities, such as interest earned on CSR funds, must be spent on CSR and does not count as part of the business's profit.
  • Impact assessment costs can be counted as CSR, within limits (see our impact assessment guide).

Common mistakes

  1. Using profit before tax instead of Section 198 net profit.
  2. Using the current year instead of the three preceding years.
  3. Forgetting to transfer unspent money on time (see unspent CSR rules).

From number to plan

Once you know the amount, the harder question is how to spend it well. A ₹4 crore obligation can fund a meaningful three-year programme in a district where CSR is scarce. In Bihar, for example, 12 of 38 districts received less than ₹1 crore of CSR each in FY 2023-24.

A company's CSR spending only counts if it goes to activities listed in Schedule VII of the Companies Act, 2013. The list is broad and interpreted liberally, but it has limits. Here is what it covers.

The main Schedule VII categories

  1. Hunger, poverty and health: eradicating hunger, poverty and malnutrition; promoting health care, including preventive care; sanitation; and safe drinking water.
  2. Education and skills: education, special education, vocational skills and livelihood projects.
  3. Gender equality and vulnerable groups: empowering women; homes and hostels for women and orphans; old age homes; and reducing inequality faced by socially and economically backward groups.
  4. Environment: environmental sustainability, ecological balance, protection of flora and fauna, animal welfare, agroforestry, conservation of natural resources and water.
  5. Heritage, art and culture: national heritage, restoration of historic sites, public libraries, and promotion of traditional arts and handicrafts.
  6. Armed forces veterans and their dependants, including war widows.
  7. Sports: training to promote rural, nationally recognised, Paralympic and Olympic sports.
  8. Government funds: contributions to the PM's National Relief Fund, PM CARES and other notified funds.
  9. Research and incubators: technology incubators in approved institutions, and certain research and development projects.
  10. Rural development projects.
  11. Slum area development.
  12. Disaster management, including relief, rehabilitation and reconstruction.

What does not count

  • Activities in the normal course of business. Limited exceptions exist for companies doing research and development on new vaccines, drugs and medical devices.
  • Activities that benefit only employees and their families.
  • Activities outside India, other than training Indian sports personnel.
  • Contributions to political parties.
  • Sponsorships for marketing benefit.
  • Activities carried out to fulfil other statutory obligations.

Choosing among the categories

Most companies concentrate on education and health, which together take the largest share of national CSR. Some of the highest-impact opportunities sit in categories that receive less attention: child nutrition, disaster resilience, safe water and women's livelihoods, especially in underserved states. Before 2021, a company that did not spend its full CSR obligation only had to explain why in its board report. Today the rules are stricter: unspent money must be either committed to ongoing projects or transferred to a government fund.

Two categories of unspent CSR

1. Money allocated to an ongoing project

An ongoing project is a multi-year project, lasting no more than three years beyond the year it starts, that the board has approved. Any amount allocated to it but not spent by year-end must be transferred to a special bank account, the Unspent CSR Account, within 30 days of the end of the financial year.

The company then has three financial years from the date of transfer to spend it. Anything left after that must go to a fund listed in Schedule VII within 30 days.

2. Money not linked to an ongoing project

Unspent CSR that is not tied to an ongoing project must be transferred to a fund listed in Schedule VII, such as the PM CARES Fund, within six months of the end of the financial year.

Penalties

Non-compliance attracts monetary penalties on the company and on officers in default, set as a multiple of the amount due, subject to caps. Penalties for CSR non-compliance are imposed in practice.

Turning unspent funds into impact

The Unspent CSR Account is often seen as a compliance burden. It can also be an opportunity: it gives companies up to three years to fund patient, multi-year programmes that need time to show results, such as child nutrition, foundational learning or flood resilience.

To use it well, companies need:

  • a clearly defined ongoing project with milestones;
  • a credible, CSR-1-registered implementing partner; and
  • a monitoring system that tracks spending against the three-year deadline.
