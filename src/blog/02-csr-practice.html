Since 1 April 2021, any organisation that implements CSR activities on behalf of a company must be registered with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs through Form CSR-1. Without it, a company cannot count the money it gives that organisation as CSR spending.

Who can register?

  • Section 8 companies, registered public trusts and registered societies that are registered under Section 12A and Section 80G of the Income Tax Act. They must also have a track record of at least three years in similar activities.
  • Section 8 companies, trusts and societies set up by the company itself, alone or with others.
  • Entities established by the central or state government, or by an Act of Parliament or a state legislature.

What is needed

Form CSR-1 is filed online on the MCA portal and must be digitally signed. It is certified by a practising chartered accountant, company secretary or cost accountant. Organisations typically need:

  • their registration certificate and PAN;
  • 12A and 80G registrations;
  • details of their directors, trustees or office-bearers; and
  • information on their track record.

Once processed, the system generates a unique CSR registration number.

Registration is the minimum, not the goal

CSR-1 makes an NGO eligible. It does not make it fundable. Companies increasingly look for:

  1. Clean governance: audited accounts, independent board members and conflict-of-interest policies.
  2. Clear outcomes: evidence of results, not just activities.
  3. Reporting capacity: the ability to produce timely utilisation certificates and progress reports.
  4. Field presence: real relationships with communities and local government.

A note for NGOs in underserved states

Grassroots organisations in states like Bihar often do excellent work but struggle to reach corporate funders. Building documentation and measurement systems, and forming consortia with peer organisations, can open the door to larger multi-year grants.

The 2021 amendments to the CSR Rules made independent impact assessment mandatory for larger CSR spenders. Done well, an assessment is far more than a compliance exercise. It is how a company learns what its money actually achieved.

Who must do an impact assessment?

A company must commission an impact assessment if:

  • its average CSR obligation is ₹10 crore or more over the three immediately preceding financial years; and
  • the project has an outlay of ₹1 crore or more and was completed at least one year before the assessment.

The assessment must be done by an independent agency, and the report is placed before the board and annexed to the annual CSR report.

Can the cost be counted as CSR?

Yes, within a limit. Spending on impact assessment can be counted as CSR up to 2% of total CSR spending for that financial year or ₹50 lakh, whichever is higher.

Outputs versus outcomes

Many assessments stop at outputs: how many children attended, how many kits were distributed. Real impact assessment asks what changed:

OutputOutcome
500 children enrolled in a reading programmeShare of class 3 children who can read a class 2 text, before and after
50 anganwadis equippedChange in stunting and underweight rates among children under 5
1,000 youth trainedShare placed in jobs, and their earnings six months later

Five principles for a good assessment

  1. Plan it on day one. A baseline taken before the project starts is essential.
  2. Choose a few meaningful indicators rather than dozens of activity counts.
  3. Use a comparison where possible, such as similar villages or schools without the programme.
  4. Listen to communities. Combine numbers with interviews and focus groups.
  5. Report honestly, including what did not work.

Across these four years, ₹770.80 crore of CSR was spent in Bihar. Private companies gave 65% of the FY 2023-24 total.

But growth is slowing

Spending almost doubled in FY 2021-22, but grew only about 8% in FY 2023-24. PSU spending actually fell, from ₹110.29 crore to ₹91.13 crore.

Bihar's share is still tiny

In FY 2023-24, ₹26,947 crore of India's CSR could be traced to a specific state. Bihar received about 1% of that, yet it is home to about 8.6% of India's population. That works out to roughly ₹25 of CSR per resident, against about ₹540 in Maharashtra and a national average of about ₹223 (using Census 2011 population).

Why this matters

Bihar faces some of India's deepest development challenges: high child stunting, the lowest literacy rate of any state, and severe flood risk across much of the north. The places where CSR could do the most good are receiving the least of it.

Bihar is India's third most populous state, yet in FY 2023-24 it received only about 1% of India's state-attributed CSR. Neighbouring Jharkhand, with roughly a third of Bihar's population, received more in absolute terms (₹414.63 crore against ₹260.53 crore). Why?

1. CSR follows corporate geography

Section 135 asks companies to give preference to the local areas around which they operate. States with many headquarters, factories and mines, such as Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka and Odisha, attract the most CSR. Bihar has relatively few large industrial bases, so fewer companies have a natural local mandate there.

2. Few investment-ready projects

CSR teams need projects that are costed, approved and ready to start, with credible implementing partners. Without a pipeline of such projects, even willing companies find it hard to commit.

3. Perceived execution risk

Companies far from Bihar may worry about monitoring, partner capacity or reporting. In practice these risks can be managed with the right partners and systems, but the perception keeps money away.

4. Much CSR is "PAN India"

In FY 2023-24, about ₹7,962 crore of national CSR was recorded as "PAN India" or not tied to any state. Some of it may reach Bihar, but there is no visibility on how much.

What could change it

  1. A state CSR facilitation cell, a single window that connects companies with projects the government has endorsed.
  2. A public CSR project bank of costed, district-wise projects.
  3. Targeted outreach to PSUs with Bihar operations and to India's largest CSR spenders.
  4. Pooled funds that let several companies co-invest in larger programmes.
  5. Transparent data published every year, so that progress can be tracked.

Muzaffarpur and Patna together received about 35% of the state's CSR.

The long tail

Twelve districts received less than ₹1 crore each in FY 2023-24. Arwal and Sheikhpura received nothing that year, and Arwal shows no recorded CSR in any of the four years from FY 2020-21. Lakhisarai (₹0.06 crore), Kaimur (₹0.10 crore), Banka (₹0.15 crore) and Sheohar (₹0.15 crore) received almost nothing.

The flood belt is left out

The seven Kosi and Seemanchal districts (Araria, Katihar, Kishanganj, Purnia, Saharsa, Supaul and Madhepura) received just ₹8.92 crore combined. That is about 3.4% of Bihar's CSR for roughly 16% of its people, in some of its most flood-prone areas.

Volatility

District figures swing sharply from year to year. Kishanganj received ₹38.36 crore in FY 2022-23 but ₹0.70 crore the year after. Pashchim Champaran fell from ₹39.73 crore in FY 2021-22 to ₹1.36 crore. Single large projects, rather than sustained programmes, explain most of these swings.
