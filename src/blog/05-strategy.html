A single company's CSR budget can rarely solve a systemic problem such as child stunting or learning poverty across a whole district. Pooled funding, where several companies co-invest in one programme, is a growing answer.

Why pool CSR funds?

  • Scale: pooled budgets can fund district- or state-wide programmes.
  • Lower costs: due diligence, monitoring and evaluation are shared.
  • Access for smaller companies: companies with small obligations can join a credible, large programme.
  • Less duplication: funders coordinate instead of crowding into the same villages.
  • Stronger government partnership: one coordinated voice is easier for administrations to work with.

How collaboratives are structured

  1. Anchor funder: one or two larger companies set the direction and make the biggest commitment.
  2. Co-funders: others join with smaller amounts, often choosing a geography or theme within the programme.
  3. Secretariat: an intermediary manages design, partners, monitoring and reporting.
  4. Shared results framework: common indicators and a single impact report.

Keeping every funder compliant

Each company must still be able to show that its own contribution went to Schedule VII activities, through CSR-1-registered implementing agencies, and must report its own spending in Form CSR-2. A well-run collaborative therefore:

  • tracks each funder's money separately;
  • issues separate utilisation certificates to each funder;
  • maps each funder's spending to specific districts and activities; and
  • supports ongoing-project rules where funds span several years.

When pooling works best

Pooling suits problems that need scale and patience: nutrition across a district, foundational learning across a block, or flood resilience across a river basin. It is less suited to small, one-off projects.

"We distributed 10,000 kits." "We trained 2,000 youth." Most CSR reports are full of numbers like these. They are outputs: what the programme did. Boards and communities increasingly want to know the outcomes: what changed as a result.

The results chain

  1. Inputs: money, staff and materials.
  2. Activities: training, distribution and construction.
  3. Outputs: the direct products, such as people trained or centres built.
  4. Outcomes: changes in knowledge, behaviour, health or income.
  5. Impact: long-term change in wellbeing at population level.

How to get there

  • Measure a baseline before starting. Without it, change cannot be shown.
  • Keep indicators few and meaningful: three good outcome measures beat thirty activity counts.
  • Collect data regularly, not only at the end, so the programme can correct course.
  • Use comparisons with similar villages or schools where possible.
  • Commission independent evaluation for larger programmes (see impact assessment rules).

CSR in India comes with significant disclosure requirements. Getting them right protects a company from penalties and builds trust with shareholders, regulators and communities.

1. The annual report on CSR

The board's report must include an annual report on CSR activities in the format set out in the CSR Rules. It covers:

  • a brief outline of the CSR policy;
  • the composition of the CSR committee;
  • the CSR obligation and the amount spent;
  • details of ongoing and other projects;
  • unspent amounts and transfers; and
  • a summary of impact assessments, where applicable.

2. Website disclosures

Companies must publish on their website the composition of the CSR committee, the CSR policy and the projects approved by the board.

3. Form CSR-2

Form CSR-2 is a separate e-form filed with the MCA that reports CSR details in a structured format. Its data feeds the government's National CSR Portal, the source of the state- and district-level statistics used in research such as our Bihar CSR Outlook Report.

Tips for accurate reporting

  1. Record every project's location at district level. Vague entries such as "PAN India" hide where money really goes.
  2. Keep utilisation certificates from every implementing agency.
  3. Reconcile with the Unspent CSR Account before filing (see unspent CSR rules).
  4. Report outcomes, not just spending, in the narrative sections.

Why location data matters

In FY 2023-24, about ₹7,962 crore of national CSR was not tied to any state, and in Bihar ₹16.41 crore was not tied to a district. Better reporting would show far more clearly where needs are being met, and where they are not.

CSR budgets are large, but small compared with what governments spend on health, education and rural development. The most effective CSR therefore does not replace government. It converges with it: filling gaps, testing new approaches and strengthening public systems.

What convergence looks like

  • CSR funds equipment and training for government anganwadis, schools and primary health centres.
  • Programmes align with national missions such as NIPUN Bharat, Poshan Abhiyaan and Jal Jeevan Mission.
  • District administrations help choose locations and track progress.
  • Successful models are adopted and scaled up by the government.

What states can do to attract CSR

Several states have set up dedicated systems to attract CSR. Common elements include:

  1. A state CSR facilitation cell: a single window for companies.
  2. An online CSR project bank: costed projects endorsed by the government.
  3. District CSR nodal officers who help companies on the ground.
  4. An annual CSR conclave where companies and the state sign MoUs.
  5. Recognition for companies that make outstanding contributions.

What companies gain

  • Faster approvals and access to government facilities.
  • Programmes that last, because the government maintains what CSR builds.
  • Credible data for impact reporting.

India's mandatory CSR regime is now more than a decade old. Total CSR spending reached about ₹40,794 crore in FY 2024-25, roughly four times the first year's figure, according to the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Here are the trends shaping CSR in 2026.

1. From projects to programmes

Companies are moving from one-year projects to multi-year programmes, helped by the ongoing-project rules and the Unspent CSR Account (see unspent CSR rules).

2. Impact assessment goes mainstream

Mandatory impact assessment for larger spenders is pushing the sector from outputs to outcomes (see outcomes vs outputs).

3. Attention on underserved geographies

CSR remains concentrated in a few industrial states. Companies, PSUs and governments are paying more attention to aspirational districts and to states like Bihar, where per-capita CSR is lowest (see CSR in Bihar).

4. Collaboratives and pooled funding

More companies are co-investing to reach scale (see pooled CSR funding).

5. Climate and resilience

Climate adaptation, including flood and heat resilience, water security and climate-smart farming, is rising up CSR agendas (see CSR for flood resilience).

6. Technology for monitoring

Live dashboards, mobile data collection and geo-tagged photos are making CSR more transparent and easier to audit.

7. Stronger partner due diligence

CSR-1 registration is now the baseline. Companies look closely at governance, finances and field capacity (see our due diligence checklist).

8. Government convergence

The most effective programmes align with public missions and work through government systems (see working with state governments).

What this means for CSR leaders

The question is shifting from "how much did we spend?" to "what did we change, and where?" Companies that answer it well will lead the next decade of CSR. Talk to Orvys about building a programme for that future.
