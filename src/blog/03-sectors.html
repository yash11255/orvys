In 2018, NITI Aayog launched the Aspirational Districts Programme to rapidly improve India's most underdeveloped districts. It tracks them on health and nutrition, education, agriculture, financial inclusion and basic infrastructure, and ranks their progress month by month.

For companies, these districts offer something rare: a clear map of where need is greatest, along with an administration motivated to show results.

Why aspirational districts suit CSR

  • Measurable baselines. Government already tracks indicators at district level, so programmes can show change against official data.
  • Engaged administrations. District Magistrates are actively looking for partners to improve their rankings.
  • High marginal impact. Each rupee reaches people with the fewest alternatives.
  • Policy alignment. CSR in these districts supports national development priorities.

Bihar's aspirational districts

Bihar has 13 aspirational districts, including Araria, Katihar, Purnia, Sitamarhi, Gaya, Nawada and Jamui. In FY 2023-24 they received about ₹110 crore of CSR in total. However, ₹52 crore of that went to Muzaffarpur alone. The other twelve averaged under ₹5 crore each, and Sheikhpura received nothing.

How to work in an aspirational district

  1. Start with the district's own data and priorities, through a meeting with the district administration.
  2. Pick one or two indicators where CSR can make a visible difference, such as anaemia or reading levels.
  3. Converge with government schemes rather than duplicating them. CSR is most powerful when it fills the gaps that schemes leave.
  4. Commit for three years. Lasting change rarely happens within a single financial year.

The first 1,000 days of life, from pregnancy to a child's second birthday, shape a lifetime of health, learning and earnings. Children who are stunted in this period carry the effects into adulthood, and the damage is largely irreversible after age two. That makes early nutrition one of the most cost-effective investments any funder can make.

The scale of the challenge in Bihar

According to the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019-21), in Bihar:

  • 42.9% of children under five are stunted;
  • 41.0% are underweight;
  • 69.4% of children aged 6-59 months are anaemic; and
  • 63.5% of women aged 15-49 are anaemic.

These are among the highest rates of any Indian state.

What CSR can fund

Strengthening anganwadi centres

Anganwadis are the front line of India's child nutrition system. CSR can equip them with weighing scales and growth charts, improve the buildings, train workers, and add early-learning materials.

Maternal nutrition and counselling

Home visits and group sessions for pregnant women and new mothers, covering diet, breastfeeding and complementary feeding, are low-cost and high-impact.

Anaemia test-and-treat

Screening adolescent girls and women, supplementing them with iron and folic acid, and tracking follow-up can reduce anaemia within a programme cycle.

Nutrition gardens and fortified food

Kitchen gardens and fortified take-home rations improve dietary diversity, especially during flood seasons, when food access drops.

Measuring what matters

Good nutrition programmes track outcomes (stunting, wasting and anaemia rates) against a baseline, not just the number of kits distributed. See outcomes versus outputs.

Education is the single largest category of CSR spending in India. Yet much of it goes to infrastructure, scholarships and digital classrooms. These are all useful, but they often miss the most important problem: many children in the early grades are not learning to read and count.

The foundational learning gap

Enrolment in India is now near-universal. But surveys have repeatedly shown that large numbers of children reach class 5 unable to read a class 2 text or solve simple arithmetic. Gaps that open in classes 1-3 widen every year after that, because the rest of the curriculum assumes children can read.

The challenge is especially acute in Bihar, which has India's lowest literacy rate: 61.8% overall and 51.5% for women (Census 2011).

NIPUN Bharat: the national goal

The government's NIPUN Bharat mission aims for every child to achieve foundational literacy and numeracy by the end of class 3. CSR programmes aligned with it can work alongside state education departments rather than in parallel.

What works

  • Teaching at the right level: grouping children by learning level rather than by grade for part of the day.
  • Structured lesson plans and materials for teachers.
  • Regular, simple assessments to track each child's progress.
  • Community and parent involvement, including reading camps and libraries.

Designing a CSR programme

  1. Choose a block and cover all its primary schools, which makes it easier to measure and partner with the government.
  2. Run a baseline assessment of reading and maths.
  3. Work through the existing teachers, not around them.
  4. Measure learning outcomes at the end of each year.

Healthcare and sanitation take one of the largest shares of India's CSR spending. Much of it funds hospital equipment in cities. But for most rural families, the health system begins and often ends at the primary health centre (PHC) and the sub-centre.

Why primary care

  • Most common illnesses, maternal care and screening can be handled at primary level.
  • Strong primary care prevents expensive hospitalisation and long journeys to cities.
  • Rural PHCs often lack diagnostics, equipment and staff, which are gaps CSR can fill quickly.

High-impact CSR health interventions

PHC and health centre upgrades

Diagnostic equipment, a basic laboratory, telemedicine links to specialists, solar power, and clean water and toilets.

Mobile medical units

Vans, or in flood-prone areas boats, that bring doctors, medicines and tests to remote villages on a fixed schedule.

Screening for non-communicable diseases

Camps for diabetes, hypertension, oral, breast and cervical cancer, and eye care, linked to follow-up treatment.

Training frontline health workers

ASHA and ANM workers are the backbone of rural health. Training and digital tools multiply their impact.

Working with the government

Health is a state subject, and PHCs are government facilities. The best CSR health programmes are designed with the district health society, so that upgrades are maintained and staffed after the project ends.

Vocational skills and livelihoods are a major Schedule VII category, and a popular one with companies. But many skilling programmes measure success by the number of people trained rather than the number who end up in decent jobs.

The migration challenge

In states like Bihar, which has India's lowest per-capita income and a very young population, millions of young people migrate each year for low-wage, informal work. Skilling that leads to stable jobs, closer to home where possible, can change family fortunes.

What makes skilling work

  1. Start with demand. Train for roles that employers are actually hiring for, ideally with hiring commitments up front.
  2. Mix technical and soft skills: workplace communication, digital literacy and financial literacy.
  3. Include on-the-job training or apprenticeships.
  4. Support people after placement, through the first months of a new job and, for those who migrate, with help settling in a new city.
  5. Track earnings at three, six and twelve months.

Beyond jobs: enterprise

For many rural households, the best livelihood is a local enterprise: agri-processing, dairy, handicrafts or services. CSR can provide training, equipment, market links and working capital through women's self-help groups. See CSR for women's enterprise.
