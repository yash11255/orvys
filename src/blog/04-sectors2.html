Every monsoon, the Kosi, Gandak, Bagmati and Mahananda rivers flood large parts of north Bihar. Homes, crops and livestock are lost, schools close and families fall back into debt. Bihar is widely described as India's most flood-prone state, and the Kosi has long been called the "Sorrow of Bihar".

Relief is not enough

Most disaster-related CSR arrives as relief after a flood: food packets, tarpaulins and medicines. Relief saves lives, but it does not reduce the damage next year. Resilience means investing before the flood, so that communities lose less and recover faster.

The funding gap

In FY 2023-24, the seven Kosi and Seemanchal districts (Araria, Katihar, Kishanganj, Purnia, Saharsa, Supaul and Madhepura) received just ₹8.92 crore of CSR combined, according to MCA data.

What resilience CSR looks like

  • Community early-warning systems: river-level alerts reaching every household through trained volunteers and mobile phones.
  • Village disaster-response teams trained in search, rescue and first aid.
  • Raised shelters with hand pumps, toilets and stored supplies, used as schools or community halls in dry months.
  • Flood-resilient housing on raised plinths.
  • Seed and fodder banks, so that farmers can replant quickly after floods.
  • Safe water: raised hand pumps and water treatment to prevent outbreaks after floods.

Climate adaptation in the south

South Bihar faces the opposite problem: drought and heatwaves in districts such as Gaya, Nawada and Aurangabad. Water harvesting, pond revival, solar irrigation and climate-smart farming are strong CSR fits there.

Eligible under Schedule VII

Disaster management, including relief, rehabilitation and reconstruction, and environmental sustainability are both Schedule VII categories.

Women's economic participation is one of the strongest drivers of family wellbeing: better nutrition, more schooling for children and greater resilience to shocks. Yet in many Indian states, few women work for pay.

A ready foundation: self-help groups

Across India, crores of women are organised into self-help groups (SHGs) under the National Rural Livelihoods Mission. In Bihar, the state's rural livelihoods mission, JEEViKA, has mobilised more than one crore women into SHGs. These groups already save, lend and meet regularly. What many lack is the support to grow from small-scale activity into real enterprises.

Where CSR can help

  1. Enterprise development: business training, product design and quality standards.
  2. Producer companies: helping SHGs federate so that they can buy inputs and sell outputs at scale.
  3. Market access: branding, packaging, e-commerce listings and links to corporate supply chains.
  4. Working capital and equipment: grants or blended finance for machinery.
  5. Childcare: crèches that free women's time for work.

Bihar's craft and food traditions

Bihar is home to world-renowned crafts such as Madhubani (Mithila) painting and Sikki grass work, and to food products such as makhana. Women-led producer groups in these value chains can earn significantly more with better design, branding and access to buyers.

Measuring empowerment

Track household income, women's own earnings, savings and decision-making, not just the number of women trained.

Piped water now reaches far more Indian households than a decade ago. But water quality remains a serious and often invisible problem. In parts of Bihar, groundwater along the Ganga contains naturally occurring arsenic above safe limits.

The arsenic problem

Long-term exposure to arsenic causes skin lesions, several cancers and damage to vital organs. In Bihar, affected areas lie mainly along the Ganga, in districts including Bhojpur, Buxar, Patna, Vaishali, Begusarai and Bhagalpur. Other parts of the state face fluoride or iron contamination, and floods regularly pollute hand pumps and wells.

Why many water projects fail

Countless water plants installed under CSR stop working within a few years. Common reasons:

  • no one is paid or trained to maintain them;
  • no budget for filters and spare parts; and
  • no community ownership.

Designing water CSR that lasts

  1. Test first. Map contamination before choosing a technology.
  2. Choose the right treatment: arsenic removal, RO or simple filtration, depending on the water.
  3. Build a business model. A local entrepreneur or SHG runs the plant and charges a small, affordable fee that covers upkeep.
  4. Monitor quality regularly, with trained village water monitors.
  5. Add WASH in institutions: safe water and toilets in schools and anganwadis.

Choosing the right implementing partner is the single biggest factor in whether a CSR programme succeeds. Here is a checklist that CSR teams can use.

1. Legal eligibility

  • Valid CSR-1 registration number (see our CSR-1 guide).
  • Current 12A and 80G registrations.
  • Registration certificate, PAN and, if it receives foreign funds, FCRA status.
  • At least three years of relevant track record.

2. Governance

  • An independent board or trustees, with no family dominance.
  • Conflict-of-interest, whistle-blower and child protection policies.
  • Regular board meetings with minutes.

3. Finances

  • Three years of audited accounts.
  • Diversified funding, not dependent on a single donor.
  • Reasonable overheads and clear financial controls.

4. Track record and results

  • Evidence of outcomes, not only activities.
  • References from other funders.
  • Any independent evaluations of past projects.

5. Field capacity

  • Staff on the ground in the target district.
  • Relationships with panchayats and the district administration.
  • Community trust: the most important factor, and best judged with a field visit.

6. Monitoring and reporting

  • The ability to produce monthly progress reports and utilisation certificates.
  • Data systems for tracking beneficiaries and outcomes.

Always visit the field

Documents tell part of the story. A day in the villages where the NGO works, talking to communities and frontline staff, tells the rest.

Every company covered by Section 135 needs a CSR policy, and its CSR committee must recommend an annual action plan to the board. Many companies treat these as formalities. Used well, they are the backbone of an effective CSR strategy.

What the CSR policy should cover

  • The company's CSR vision and priority themes.
  • Preferred geographies, including local areas of operation.
  • How projects are selected, approved and implemented.
  • Monitoring and impact assessment approach.
  • Governance: roles of the board, the CSR committee and management.

What the annual action plan must include

Under the CSR Rules, the annual action plan should list:

  1. the projects or programmes to be undertaken, within Schedule VII;
  2. how they will be executed, directly or through implementing agencies;
  3. how funds will be used and on what schedule;
  4. monitoring and reporting mechanisms;
  5. details of any impact assessment needed; and
  6. whether any project is an "ongoing project".

From compliance to strategy

Focus

Companies that spread small amounts across many causes rarely achieve visible impact. Choosing one to three themes and building depth works better.

Geography

Look beyond headquarters. Underserved districts offer far greater impact per rupee. In Bihar, CSR per resident is about ₹25, against a national average of about ₹223.

Multi-year thinking

Use ongoing projects to commit for three years, so that programmes have time to show results.

Evidence

Build baselines and outcome indicators into the plan from the start.
